Guidelines

The final project is intended to give you an opportunity to explore one or more ideas from the course. Students can work either individually or in small groups consisting of 2-3 members. Pretty much anything to do with reinforcement should be acceptable, as long as the difficulty level is reasonable. 


Proposal

You must submit a one page project proposal by the date listed above, using the Canvas system. This written proposal should describe the proposed project in enough detail that both you and the instructor know fairly precisely what is to be done, and what principles from the course that will be emphasized. Submit the proposal as a webpage or pdf. We will have the chance to discuss the proposals in class and/or in person and/or via email, and agree on specifics details. 


Submitting the Project

Submit your presentation, report, and code by the due dates via Canvas. Your report should be in the US Letter IEEE format. The report should follow the following format: Introduction (motivation, problem overview), Methodology (detailed description of the approach taken), Results, Code Status & Resources Used (things that are working, issues/bug of the current implementation, etc.), Conclusion and Future Directions. 


Presentation

All final projects should be accompanied by a presentation. In particular, you need to prepare a 5-7 min video presentation. The presentations will be played in class during the during the scheduled day of the exams (12/6). 


Project Suggestions

Ideally, your final project explores an interesting new idea and could even serve as the basis for a future paper submission. Projects may involve applying a state-of-the-art method to a new domain, extending an existing approach, or conducting an in-depth empirical evaluation of an important RL concept. You are encouraged to be original and to choose a topic that genuinely interests you. If you get stuck, the course staff will gladly help you brainstorm and shape your ideas into a feasible and exciting project. 


Example open-ended project themes:

Curriculum Learning – design or analyze curriculum strategies for policy gradient methods.

Multi-Agent RL – explore cooperative or adversarial settings, compare single-agent vs. multi-agent methods, or implement self-play.

Hierarchical RL – investigate hierarchical policy structures or policy chaining. For example, train separate locomotion primitive policies and combine them for navigation-based control.

Multi-Objective RL – study approaches to combine or balance multiple (possibly conflicting) reward terms during policy optimization.

RL from Human Feedback – compare techniques for leveraging expert feedback or demonstration data in policy gradient or value-based algorithms.

World Models – learn and exploit environment models to improve planning and sample efficiency. For example, train a predictive model of environment transitions and use it for imagination rollouts, or policy regularization.

Framework and Systems Projects – port existing RL frameworks to new simulation engines (e.g., from PyBullet to MuJoCo, or to JAX), or improve training pipelines for efficiency and reproducibility.


The above topics are only suggestions.  We encourage you to propose your own ideas. We may also have some readily available projects for teams who prefer more structure.
